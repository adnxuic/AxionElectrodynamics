\section{The theta term and Axion Electrodynamics}

In relativistic notation, the Maxwell action for electromagnetism takes a wonderfully compact form:
\begin{equation}
S_{Max} = \int \mathrm{d}^4x -\frac{1}{4} F^{\mu\nu} F_{\mu\nu} = \int \mathrm{d}^4x \frac{1}{2} (\bm{E}^2 - \bm{B}^2)
\end{equation}
Here:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
    F_{\mu\nu} &= \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu \\
        & = \begin{pmatrix}
            0 & -E_x & -E_y & -E_z \\
            E_x & 0 & -B_z & B_y \\
            E_y & B_z & 0 & -B_x \\
            E_z & -B_y & B_x & 0
            \end{pmatrix}\\
    E_i &= F_{0i} \\
    F_{ij} &= -\epsilon_{ijk} B_k
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}
One reason the Maxwell action is so simple is that there is very little else we can write down that is both gauge-invariant and Lorentz-invariant. 
There is, however, one additional term that we can add to the Maxwell action that is also gauge-invariant and Lorentz-invariant; we call it the theta term:
\begin{equation}
S_\theta = \frac{\theta e^2}{4\mathrm{\pi}^2} \int \mathrm{d}^4x \frac{1}{4} {}^\star F^{\mu\nu} F_{\mu\nu} = -\frac{\theta e^2}{4\mathrm{\pi}^2} \int \mathrm{d}^4x \bm{E} \cdot \bm{B}
\end{equation}
where ${}^\star F^{\mu\nu}$ is the dual tensor:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    {}^\star F^{\mu\nu} &= \frac{1}{2} \epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} F_{\rho\sigma} \\
    &= \begin{pmatrix}
        0 & -B_x & -B_y & -B_z \\
        B_x & 0 & E_z & -E_y \\
        B_y & -E_z & 0 & E_x \\
        B_z & E_y & -E_x & 0
        \end{pmatrix}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\theta$ a constant parameter.

However, it is straightforward to check that the theta term can be written as a total derivative:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\int \mathrm{d}^4x \frac{1}{4} {}^\star F^{\mu\nu} F_{\mu\nu} &= \frac{1}{2} \int \mathrm{d}^4x \cdot \frac{1}{4} \epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} F_{\rho\sigma} F_{\mu\nu} \\
&= \frac{1}{8} \int \mathrm{d}^4x \cdot \epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} (\partial_\rho A_\sigma - \partial_\sigma A_\rho) (\partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu) \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \int \mathrm{d}^4x \cdot \epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} (\partial_\mu A_\nu \partial_\rho A_\sigma)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Now let us prove a useful identity:
\begin{equation}
\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} A_\nu \partial_\mu \partial_\rho A_\sigma = 0
\end{equation}
because:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} A_\nu \partial_\mu \partial_\rho A_\sigma &= \epsilon^{\rho\nu\mu\sigma} A_\nu \partial_\rho \partial_\mu A_\sigma \\
&= \epsilon^{\rho\nu\mu\sigma} A_\nu \partial_\mu \partial_\rho A_\sigma \\
&= -\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} A_\nu \partial_\mu \partial_\rho A_\sigma
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Therefore, we obtain:
\begin{equation}
2\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} A_\nu \partial_\mu \partial_\rho A_\sigma = 0 \Rightarrow \epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} A_\nu \partial_\mu \partial_\rho A_\sigma = 0
\end{equation}

Thus, the quadratic part of the theta term becomes:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\int \mathrm{d}^4x \frac{1}{4} {}^\star F^{\mu\nu} F_{\mu\nu} &= \frac{1}{2} \int \mathrm{d}^4x \epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} (\partial_\mu A_\nu \partial_\rho A_\sigma + A_\nu \partial_\mu \partial_\rho A_\sigma) \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \int \mathrm{d}^4x \epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} \partial_\mu (A_\nu \partial_\rho A_\sigma)
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Therefore, the theta term can be written as:
\begin{equation}
S_\theta = \frac{\theta e^2}{8\mathrm{\pi}^2} \int \mathrm{d}^4x \partial_\mu (\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma} A_\nu \partial_\rho A_\sigma)
\end{equation}
We therefore say that the theta term is topological: it depends only on boundary data. 
However, when deriving the equations of motion via the principle of least action, 
the fields are held fixed at the boundary (at infinity). 
Therefore, the theta term does not modify the classical equations of motion and, at first sight, seems to have little physical effect.

But in situations involving a subtle interplay between quantum mechanics and topology, 
the theta term leads to a number of interesting physical phenomena.

We can also consider situations in which $\theta$ affects the dynamics classically. This occurs when $\theta$ is not constant, but instead varies in space and time.

\begin{equation}
\theta = \theta(\bm{x}, t)
\end{equation}

We can then write down the action for axion electrodynamics:
\begin{equation}
S = \int \mathrm{d}^4x (-\frac{1}{4} F^{\mu\nu} F_{\mu\nu} + \frac{e^2}{16\mathrm{\pi}^2} \theta(\bm{x}, t) {}^\star F^{\mu\nu} F_{\mu\nu})
\end{equation}

We use the Euler--Lagrange equations:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial A_\mu} - \partial_\nu (\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\nu A_\mu)}) = 0
\end{equation}

to obtain the equations of motion for axion electrodynamics.

Since $\mathcal{L}$ does not depend explicitly on $A_\mu$, the equations of motion reduce to:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial A_\mu} = 0 \Rightarrow \partial_\nu [\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\nu A_\mu)}] = 0
\end{equation}

and the Lagrangian density for axion electrodynamics is $(\alpha = \frac{e^2}{4\mathrm{\pi}})$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L} &= \mathcal{L}_{Max} + \mathcal{L}_\theta \\
&= -\frac{1}{4} F^{\mu\nu} F_{\mu\nu} + \frac{\alpha}{4\mathrm{\pi}} \theta(\bm{x}, t) {}^\star F^{\mu\nu} F_{\mu\nu}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

First, let us consider $\mathcal{L}_\theta$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_\nu A_\mu)} &= \frac{\partial}{\partial (\partial_\nu A_\mu)} \cdot \frac{\alpha}{4\mathrm{\pi}} \theta(\bm{x}, t) \cdot \frac{1}{2} \epsilon^{\alpha\beta\rho\sigma} F_{\alpha\beta} F_{\rho\sigma} \\
&= \frac{\alpha}{8\mathrm{\pi}} \{ [\frac{\partial}{\partial (\partial_\nu A_\mu)} \theta(\bm{x}, t)] \cdot \epsilon^{\alpha\beta\rho\sigma} F_{\alpha\beta} F_{\rho\sigma} + \theta(\bm{x}, t) [\frac{\partial \epsilon^{\alpha\beta\rho\sigma} F_{\alpha\beta} F_{\rho\sigma}}{\partial (\partial_\nu A_\mu)}] \}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Let us first consider the first term:
\begin{equation}
\epsilon^{\alpha\beta\rho\sigma} F_{\alpha\beta} F_{\rho\sigma} \frac{\partial}{\partial (\partial_\nu A_\mu)} \theta(\bm{x}, t) = 0
\end{equation}

Next, consider the second term:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\theta(\bm{x}, t) \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial (\partial_\nu A_\mu)} \cdot \epsilon^{\alpha\beta\rho\sigma} F_{\alpha\beta} F_{\rho\sigma} &= 4 \theta(\bm{x}, t) (\frac{\partial}{\partial (\partial_\nu A_\mu)} \partial_\alpha A_\beta \partial_\rho A_\sigma) \epsilon^{\alpha\beta\rho\sigma} \\
&= 4 \theta(\bm{x}, t) (\delta^\nu_\alpha \delta^\mu_\beta \partial_\rho A_\sigma + \delta^\nu_\rho \delta^\mu_\sigma \partial_\alpha A_\beta) \cdot \epsilon^{\alpha\beta\rho\sigma} \\
&= 4 \theta(\bm{x}, t) \cdot \epsilon^{\nu\mu\rho\sigma} (\partial_\rho A_\sigma - \partial_\sigma A_\rho) \\
&= 8 \theta(\bm{x}, t) {}^\star F^{\nu\mu}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Next, we have:
\begin{equation}
\partial_\nu [\theta(\bm{x}, t) {}^\star F^{\nu\mu}] = {}^\star F^{\nu\mu} \partial_\nu \theta(\bm{x}, t) + \theta(\bm{x}, t) \cdot \partial_\nu {}^\star F^{\nu\mu}
\end{equation}

Using the Bianchi identity:
\begin{equation}
\partial_\mu {}^\star F^{\mu\nu} = 0
\end{equation}

so we obtain:
\begin{equation}
\partial_\nu \theta(\bm{x}, t) {}^\star F^{\nu\mu} = {}^\star F^{\nu\mu} \partial_\nu \theta(\bm{x}, t)
\end{equation}

Finally, we can get:
\begin{equation}
\partial_\nu [\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_\theta}{\partial (\partial_\nu A_\mu)}] = \frac{\alpha}{\mathrm{\pi}} {}^\star F^{\nu\mu} \cdot \partial_\nu \theta(\bm{x}, t)
\end{equation}

For the Maxwell term, we have:
\begin{equation}
\partial_\nu [\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{Max}}{\partial (\partial_\nu A_\mu)}] = - \partial_\nu F^{\nu\mu}
\end{equation}

Therefore, the equations of motion for axion electrodynamics are:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\partial_\nu F^{\nu\mu} &= \frac{\alpha}{\mathrm{\pi}} {}^\star F^{\nu\mu} \cdot \partial_\nu \theta(\bm{x}, t) \\
\partial_\mu {}^\star F^{\mu\nu} &= 0
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

In terms of the electric and magnetic fields, the modified Maxwell equations (axion electrodynamics) are:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\nabla \cdot \bm{E} &= \frac{\alpha}{\mathrm{\pi}} (\nabla \theta) \cdot \bm{B} \\
\nabla \times \bm{B} &= \frac{\partial \bm{E}}{\partial t} - \frac{\alpha}{\mathrm{\pi}} (\dot{\theta} \bm{B} + \nabla \theta \times \bm{E}) \\
\nabla \cdot \bm{B} &= 0 \\
\nabla \times \bm{E} &= - \frac{\partial \bm{B}}{\partial t}
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

The first equation tells us that in regions of space where $\theta$ varies, a magnetic field $\bm{B}$ acts like an electric charge density. The second equation tells us that the combination $(\dot{\theta}\bm{B} + \nabla\theta \times \bm{E})$ acts like a current density.

\subsection{The periodicity of theta and its values under parity or time-reversal symmetry}

In quantum theory, $\theta$ is a periodic variable: it lies in the range:
\begin{equation}
\theta \in [0, 2\mathrm{\pi})
\end{equation}
After imposing appropriate boundary conditions, $S_\theta$ can only take values of the form:
\begin{equation}
S_\theta = \theta N \quad \text{with } N \in \mathbb{Z}
\end{equation}
This means that the theta angle contributes to the partition function as:
\begin{equation}
e^{\mathrm{i}S_\theta} = e^{\mathrm{i}N\theta}
\end{equation}
To show that $S_\theta$ must take this form, we consider a compact Euclidean spacetime, which we take to be $T^4$, with each circle of the torus having radius $R$.

We consider the simple case:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\bm{E} &= (0, 0, E_z), \ E_z = F_{03} = \partial_0 A_3 - \partial_3 A_0 \\
\bm{B} &= (0, 0, B_z), \ B_z = F_{21} = \partial_2 A_1 - \partial_1 A_2
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}
The relevant integral in $S_\theta$ is:
\begin{equation}
I = \int_{T^4} \mathrm{d}^4x E_z \cdot B_z = \int_{T^4} \mathrm{d}x^0 \mathrm{d}x^3 \cdot E_z \cdot \int_{T^4} \mathrm{d}x^1 \mathrm{d}x^2 \cdot B_z
\end{equation}
The gauge field $A_\mu$ must be well defined on the underlying torus, which will put restrictions on the allowed values of $E_z$ and $B_z$.
Thus, the integral cannot take an arbitrary value.

Now let us consider the restrictions on $A_\mu$ required for it to be well defined on the torus. When a spatial direction, say $x^1$, is periodic with radius $R$, the physical state at the initial point ($x^1=0$) must be identical to that at the final point:
\begin{equation}
\text{state}[A_1(0)] = \text{state}[A_1(2\mathrm{\pi} R)]
\end{equation}
Since $B_z = \partial_2 A_1$ and (taking $A_2=0$) $A_2$ is constant, we have $A_1 = B_z x^2 + C$. The simplest choice $A_1=0$ is trivial. Therefore, we should find a gauge transformation that connects $A_1(0)$ and $A_1(2\mathrm{\pi} R)$, so that two different values of $A_1$ correspond to the same physical state.

\begin{equation}
A_1(2\mathrm{\pi} R) = A_1(0) + G
\end{equation}
Let us find the gauge transformation of interest:
\begin{equation}
A_1 \to A_1 + \partial_1 \chi
\end{equation}
We do not require $\chi(x)$ to be single valued. Instead, we require only that $e^{\mathrm{i}e\chi}$ be single-valued, since this is what acts on the wavefunction. Therefore, $\chi$ can take the simple form:
\begin{equation}
\chi = \frac{n x^1}{e R} \quad \text{with } n \in \mathbb{Z}
\end{equation}
Because at the same point ($2\mathrm{\pi} NR + X$), $e^{\mathrm{i}e\chi}$ represents the same state.

These are sometimes called large gauge transformations, a name which reflects the fact that they cannot be continuously deformed to the identity. Under such a gauge transformation, we have:
\begin{equation}
A_1 \to A_1 + \partial_1 \chi = A_1 + \frac{n}{eR}
\end{equation}
The two different values of $A_1$ correspond to the same physical state. From this transformation, we can determine the expression for $A_1$:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
A_1(0) &= A_1(2\mathrm{\pi} R) + \frac{n}{eR} \\
\Rightarrow A_1 &= \frac{n x^2}{2\mathrm{\pi} e R^2}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
We can then write down the expression for $B_z$:
\begin{equation}
B_z = \partial_2 A_1 = \frac{n}{2\mathrm{\pi} e R^2}
\end{equation}
Therefore, the integral of the magnetic field is:
\begin{equation}
\int_{T^2} \mathrm{d}x^1 \mathrm{d}x^2 B_z = \frac{2\mathrm{\pi} n}{e} \quad \text{with } n \in \mathbb{Z}
\end{equation}
We can also apply exactly the same argument to the electric field.
\begin{equation}
E_z = \partial_0 A_3 \quad (A_0 = 0)
\end{equation}
So the integral of electric field is:
\begin{equation}
\int_{T^2} \mathrm{d}x^0 \mathrm{d}x^3 E_z = \frac{2\mathrm{\pi} n'}{e} \quad \text{with } n' \in \mathbb{Z}
\end{equation}
Armed with two integrals above, the $S_\theta$ becomes:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{aligned}
    S_\theta &= -\frac{\theta e^2}{4\mathrm{\pi}^2} \int_{T^4} \mathrm{d}^4x \bm{E} \cdot \bm{B} \\
    &= -\frac{\theta e^2}{4\mathrm{\pi}^2} \frac{4\mathrm{\pi}^2}{e^2} \cdot n n' \quad (\text{let } N = -nn') \\
    &= N \theta \quad \text{with } N \in \mathbb{Z}
    \end{aligned}
\end{equation}

It is straightforward to generalise this to non-constant $\bm{E}$ and $\bm{B}$ fields:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\int_{T^2} \mathrm{d}x^1 \mathrm{d}x^2 B_z &= \int_{T^2} \mathrm{d}x^1 \mathrm{d}x^2 \cdot \partial_2 A_1 \\
&= 2\mathrm{\pi} R [A_1(2\mathrm{\pi} R) - A_1(0)] \\
&= \frac{2\mathrm{\pi} n}{e}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Therefore, $\theta$ is a periodic variable in quantum theory.

Although the theta term is gauge-invariant and Lorentz-invariant, it is not invariant under certain discrete symmetries 
and therefore does not preserve the same symmetries as the Maxwell term. These discrete symmetries are parity $\mathcal{P}$ and time reversal $\mathcal{T}$.

\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{P}: \bm{x} &\to -\bm{x} \\
\mathcal{T}: t &\to -t
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

If we act on both $\bm{E}$ and $\bm{B}$, we have

\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{P}: \bm{E}(\bm{x}, t) &\to -\bm{E}(-\bm{x}, t) \text{ and } \mathcal{P}: \bm{B}(\bm{x}, t) \to \bm{B}(-\bm{x}, t) \\
\mathcal{T}: \bm{E}(\bm{x}, t) &\to \bm{E}(\bm{x}, -t) \text{ and } \mathcal{T}: \bm{B}(\bm{x}, t) \to -\bm{B}(\bm{x}, -t)
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

So $\bm{E}$ is odd under parity and even under time reversal. $\bm{B}$ is even under parity and odd under time reversal.

This means that the theta term $S_\theta \propto \int \mathrm{d}^4x \bm{E} \cdot \bm{B}$ breaks both parity and time-reversal invariance: 
one finds $S_\theta \to -S_\theta$ under $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$. However, because of the periodicity of $\theta$, there are two exceptions:

\begin{equation}
S_\theta = -S_\theta + 2\mathrm{\pi} N
\end{equation}

To make this equation hold for any integer value of $N$, $\theta$ must be equal to $0$ or $\mathrm{\pi}$ ($\theta \in [0, 2\mathrm{\pi})$).

Therefore, when $\theta=0$ or $\theta=\mathrm{\pi}$, the theory is invariant under $\mathcal{P}$ and $\mathcal{T}$ symmetries.

In subsequent sections, we will see that a topological insulator with a $\mathbb{Z}_2$ classification has $\theta=\mathrm{\pi}$ because such materials are defined to be time-reversal invariant. Most materials have $\theta=0$. However, some materials have a band structure that is twisted in a particular way, which results in $\theta=\mathrm{\pi}$.

\subsection{Continuity Conditions}

Let us consider an equivalent way to describe the physics above. We introduce the electric displacement:
\begin{equation}
\bm{D} = \bm{E} - \frac{\alpha \theta}{\uppi} \cdot \bm{B}
\end{equation}
Because the divergence of $\bm{B}$ is equal to zero, we have:
\begin{equation}
\nabla \bm{D} = \nabla \bm{E} - \frac{\alpha}{\uppi} (\nabla \cdot \theta) \bm{B} = 0.
\end{equation}
Comparing with the expression for $\bm{D}$, we see that a magnetic field $\bm{B}$ acts like a polarisation in a topological insulator. When $\theta$ varies, we obtain a spatially varying polarisation, which results in bound charge. Similarly, we define the magnetising field:
\begin{equation}
\bm{H} = \bm{B} + \frac{\alpha}{\uppi} \theta \cdot \bm{E}
\end{equation}
Using the relationship between $\bm{B}$ and $\bm{E}$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial \bm{B}}{\partial t} + \nabla \times \bm{E} = 0
\end{equation}

So we obtain:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\nabla \times \bm{H} &= \nabla \times \bm{B} + \frac{\alpha}{\uppi} [ (\nabla \theta) \times \bm{E} + \theta (\nabla \times \bm{E}) ] \\
&= \frac{\partial \bm{D}}{\partial t} + \frac{\alpha}{\uppi} \theta \cdot (\frac{\partial \bm{B}}{\partial t} + \nabla \times \bm{E}) \\
&= \frac{\partial \bm{D}}{\partial t}
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
In a topological insulator, $\bm{E}$ acts like a magnetisation. When $\theta$ varies, we obtain a spatially varying magnetisation, which results in bound currents.

With these definitions, the equations of axion electrodynamics take the usual form of Maxwell equations in matter:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&\nabla \cdot \bm{D} = 0 \\
&\nabla \times \bm{H} - \frac{\partial \bm{D}}{\partial t} = 0 \\
&\nabla \cdot \bm{B} = 0 \\
&\frac{\partial \bm{B}}{\partial t} + \nabla \times \bm{E} = 0
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}
This means that if we introduce the surface normal vector $\bm{\hat{n}}$, we have the boundary conditions:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&\bm{\hat{n}} \cdot \Delta \bm{D} = 0 \\
&\bm{\hat{n}} \times \Delta \bm{E} = 0 \\
&\bm{\hat{n}} \cdot \Delta \bm{B} = 0 \\
&\bm{\hat{n}} \times \Delta \bm{H} = 0
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Witten effect}

The effect of the $\theta$ term is to endow a magnetic monopole with an electric charge; this is known as the Witten effect.

Let us take a magnetic monopole with magnetic charge $g$ and place it inside a vacuum ($\theta=0$). We then surround it with a medium that has $\theta = \Theta \neq 0$.

When the magnetic field crosses the interface where $\theta$ changes, it will induce an electric charge:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\int \nabla \cdot \bm{E} \mathrm{d} v = \int \frac{\alpha}{\uppi} (\nabla \cdot \theta) \cdot \bm{B} \mathrm{d} v. \\
&\Rightarrow \quad Q \quad = \frac{\alpha}{\uppi} \Theta g
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This result is independent of the size of the interior region where $\theta$ is zero. We could therefore shrink this region down until it is infinitesimally small, and we would still find that the monopole carries charge $q$. In fact, when $\theta$ is not equal to zero, a monopole is a dyon that carries both magnetic and electric charge.

When the monopole carries the minimum allowed magnetic charge, its electric charge is given by:
\begin{equation}
g = \frac{2 \uppi}{e} \Rightarrow q = \frac{e \theta}{2 \uppi}
\end{equation}
In particular, if we place a magnetic monopole inside a topological insulator, it turns into a dyon that carries half the charge of an electron.

We can also consider placing a monopole in a medium whose $\theta$ will gradually increase from zero. At the end of this process, the final electric field will be:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
&\int_0^{t_0} \frac{\partial \bm{E}}{\partial t} \mathrm{d} t = \frac{\alpha}{\uppi} \int_0^{t_0} \dot{\theta} \bm{B} \\
&\Rightarrow \quad \bm{E} \quad = \frac{\alpha}{\uppi} \Theta \bm{B} \quad [\text{which } \theta(t_0)=\Theta] \\
&\Rightarrow \quad q \quad = \frac{\alpha}{\uppi} \Theta g
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\subsection{A Mirage Monopole}

Let us consider an electric charge $q$ placed in the vacuum at the point $\bm{x}=(0,0,d)$. We then fill the region where $z$ is less than zero with a topological insulator. One finds that the effect produced by the surface charge is equivalent to placing mirror dyons at the points $\bm{x}=(0,0,d)$ and $\bm{x}'=(0,0,-d)$.

We assume that the mirror dyon at $\bm{x}=(0,0,d)$ has electric and magnetic charges $(q_1, g_1)$ and that the mirror dyon at $\bm{x}'=(0,0,-d)$ has electric and magnetic charges $(q_2, g_2)$.

Next, we write:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&\bm{E} = -\nabla \phi \quad (\text{because } \nabla \times \bm{E} = 0) \\
&\bm{B} = -\nabla \Omega \quad (\text{because } \nabla \times \bm{B} = 0)
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

The electric potential in the two regions is:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&\phi_+ = \frac{1}{4\uppi} \left[ \frac{q}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z-d)^2}} + \frac{q_2}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z+d)^2}} \right] , & z > 0 \\
&\phi_- = \frac{1}{4\uppi} \frac{q+q_1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z-d)^2}} , & z < 0
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

By symmetry, we only need to consider the electric and magnetic fields in the $yz$ plane at $z=0$.
\begin{equation}
    \left\{
    \begin{aligned}
        & \left. 
        \begin{aligned}
            E_y^+ &= -\partial_y \phi_+ = \frac{q+q_2}{4\pi} \cdot \frac{y}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} \\
            E_z^+ &= -\partial_z \phi_+ = \frac{q_2-q}{4\pi} \cdot \frac{d}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
        \end{aligned} 
        \right\} z=0^+ \\[10pt]
        & \left. 
        \begin{aligned}
            E_y^- &= -\partial_y \phi_- = \frac{q+q_1}{4\pi} \cdot \frac{y}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} \\
            E_z^- &= -\partial_z \phi_- = \frac{-(q+q_1)}{4\pi} \cdot \frac{d}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
        \end{aligned} 
        \right\} z=0^-
    \end{aligned}
    \right.
\end{equation}

Using the boundary conditions:
\begin{equation}
\bm{\hat{n}} \times \bm{E} = 0
\end{equation}

So we obtain:
\begin{equation}
E_y^+ = E_y^- \Rightarrow q_1 = q_2 = q'
\end{equation}

We can perform the same procedure for the magnetic field.
The magnetic scalar potential is:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&\Omega_+ = \frac{1}{4\uppi} \cdot \frac{g_2}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z+d)^2}} , & z > 0 \\
&\Omega_- = \frac{1}{4\uppi} \cdot \frac{g_1}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z-d)^2}} , & z < 0
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

Thus, the magnetic field in the $yz$ plane at $z=0$ is:
\begin{equation}
    \left\{
    \begin{aligned}
        & \left. 
        \begin{aligned}
            B_y^+ &= -\partial_y \Omega_+ = \frac{g_2}{4\pi} \cdot \frac{y}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} \\
            B_z^+ &= -\partial_z \Omega_+ = \frac{g_2}{4\pi} \cdot \frac{d}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
        \end{aligned} 
        \right\} z=0^+ \\[10pt]
        & \left. 
        \begin{aligned}
            B_y^- &= -\partial_y \Omega_- = \frac{g_1}{4\pi} \cdot \frac{y}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} \\
            B_z^- &= -\partial_z \Omega_- = \frac{-g_1}{4\pi} \frac{d}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
        \end{aligned} 
        \right\} z=0^-
    \end{aligned}
    \right.
\end{equation}

Using the boundary conditions:
\begin{equation}
\bm{\hat{n}} \cdot \bm{B} = 0 \Rightarrow B_z^- = B_z^+ \Rightarrow g_2 = -g_1 = g
\end{equation}

From the expression above, we obtain the electric displacement:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&D_z^+ = E_z^+ = \frac{q'-q}{4\uppi} \cdot \frac{d}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} \\
&D_z^- = E_z^- - \frac{\alpha}{\uppi} \theta B_z^- = \frac{-(q+q')+\alpha g}{4\uppi} \cdot \frac{d}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

Using the boundary conditions:
\begin{equation}
\bm{\hat{n}} \cdot \bm{D} = 0 \Rightarrow D_z^+ = D_z^- \Rightarrow g = \frac{-2q'}{\alpha}
\end{equation}

The magnetising field:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&H_y^+ = B_y^+ = \frac{g}{4\uppi} \cdot \frac{y}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}} \\
&H_y^- = B_y^- + \frac{\alpha}{\uppi} \theta E_y^- = \frac{-g+\alpha(q+q')}{4\uppi} \frac{y}{(x^2+y^2+d^2)^{\frac{3}{2}}}
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}

Using the boundary condition:
\begin{equation}
\nabla \times \bm{H} = 0 \Rightarrow H_y^+ = H_y^- \Rightarrow g = \frac{\alpha(q+q')}{2}
\end{equation}

Finally, we obtain the electric and magnetic charges carried by these dyons:
\begin{equation}
\left\{
\begin{aligned}
&q' = \frac{-\alpha^2}{4+\alpha^2} \cdot q \\
&g = \frac{2\alpha}{4+\alpha^2} \cdot q
\end{aligned}
\right.
\end{equation}
